The judge, not the layer, decided the result

A one-night run of the cheapest-layer ladder: three places to store a lesson, two judges. No learned layer beat a one-line prompt change, and the two judges barely agreed on which output was better.

Kyle, for Coframe, October 2026 · companion to Learn where labels are cheap

What I ran

  • A generator writes a headline, subhead and CTA for a site brief: audience, goal, voice, and claims it must not make.
  • 12 synthetic briefs: 8 train, 4 held-out. Held-out briefs never reach an update step. The judge-calibration items use only train briefs, though the decision to switch judges was prompted by run 1’s ceiling.
  • The generator is deliberately cheap (Llama 3.1 8B) so there is room to improve. Everything runs on Cloudflare Workers AI through AI Gateway, and every request is tagged so the gateway log joins back to the run.
  • A rubric judge scores brand fit, specificity, claim safety and goal fit, 1–5 each, at temperature 0.
3 layers + baseline4 learning iterations each, scored on train and held-out after every one
2 full runs + 2 controlsSame generator and code; only the judge changes. Controls: a static rubric-in-prompt baseline, and cross-judging every final output
totalRun 1 , run 2 , calibration , controls , one aborted attempt

Code and every artifact: cll/ in the repo. Run 1: model calls, all successful; the AI Gateway log (which also holds the calibration calls) joins back to every one. Scale is small on purpose: this design can only detect a lift of about – points.

Both judges order known items correctly. They disagree on where real output sits.

  • 16 items on train briefs: 5 good, 7 with one planted defect, 4 plausible but generic. Every judge ranks all good items above all generic ones, and gives the targeted criterion a 1 on every defect.
  • The difference is level, not order. The 70B puts generic copy at 3.56 and the generator’s real outputs near its “good” anchor (~4.7). DeepSeek puts generic at 2.50 and real outputs in between (~3.7).
  • Temperature 0 is not deterministic on served models: the 70B’s repeats were identical on all 16 items, DeepSeek’s differed on 9. Even so, judge repeats explain only about 15% of the variance of a 4-brief mean in run 2; generation and brief-to-brief variance dominate.

Leniency and compression in rubric judges: consistent with Thakur et al. 2024. A Llama judge scoring Llama output may also prefer it (Panickssery et al. 2024): a hypothesis here, not tested. Temp-0 nondeterminism: Atil et al. 2024. I wrote the calibration labels, so the hand scores on slide 9 are the independent check.

For a bandit, the judge’s job is to order variants near the top. Two judges that each order the planted calibration items perfectly can still disagree on real, mid-quality outputs. That is where an ideation loop actually operates.

What failed

What happenedWhat it means

Run 1: the 8B reflector returned hero copy, or prompts locked to one train brief. A gym advert used as the system prompt scored within 0.08 of the real prompt on train.

The gate rejected all four, but three by less than one pass’s noise. That is a judge-resolution problem more than a gate success.

Small optimisers struggle with the meta-task (consistent with Zhang et al. 2024 on OPRO). Strong self-reflection results such as ACE use one large model in every role, with informative feedback.

Here the reflector was too small and the feedback too thin (“could be more dynamic”). Fix both before blaming the layer.

The lessons mostly re-derived the rubric: be specific, avoid absolute claims. One recommended urgency and scarcity, which pushes against claim safety.

The missing control is a static prompt that already contains the rubric and forbidden claims.

A fixed 4.0 bar let all 8 train outputs into the few-shot bank under the 70B, format breakers included.

An absolute threshold imports the judge’s leniency. Use a per-judge percentile.

What this suggests for Coframe’s loop

A judge can be right about order and useless near the top
→
Measure resolution where variants actually sit, not average agreement
A lift appeared under one judge only
→
Evaluate with a different judge from the one that gives feedback
A one-line spec beat every learned layer
→
Put known constraints in the prompt or in code before asking a loop to learn them
Judge repeats explained ~15% of variance
→
Spend calls on more generations, briefs, or a cross-family panel (Verga et al. 2024)
Format and banned claims slipped past a rubric judge
→
Check hard constraints in code before any judge or traffic

All of this is the first deck’s thesis, that the evaluator caps the loop, at its smallest scale. Do not read a layer ranking from it; the ranking flipped with the judge.

Reproduce it

  1. Put CF_ACCOUNT_ID, CF_GATEWAY_NAME and CF_API_TOKEN in cll/.env.
  2. ./run.sh --dry prints calls, dollars and neurons without spending anything.
  3. JUDGE_MODEL=@cf/deepseek-ai/deepseek-v4-pro-0813 ./run.sh --judge-n 2 runs everything: plot, gain table, edit log, blind hand-score sheet and gateway trace.
  4. python -m ladder.calibrate MODEL… tests a judge before you trust it.
Held-out discipline: held-out briefs never reach update(), the judge choice or the calibration set.
